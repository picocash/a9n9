A post in an online Gen X forum described watching old 1990s concert footage and noticing what was missing: a crowd of faces, and not one screen held up between them and the stage.

The psychologist Linda Henkel has studied what happens when the screen goes up. "People so often whip out their cameras almost mindlessly to capture a moment," she said in 2013, when her study at Fairfield University was published in *Psychological Science*.

## What the camera does to memory

Henkel took students on a tour of the university's Bellarmine Museum of Art. They photographed some objects and simply looked at others. Tested the next day, they recognised fewer of the objects they had photographed and remembered fewer of their details. She called it the photo-taking impairment effect.

There was a twist. In a second study, people who zoomed in on one detail remembered the whole object better, including parts outside the frame. Paying close attention seemed to protect the memory. "These results show how the 'mind's eye' and the camera's eye are not the same," Henkel said.

A museum is not a mosh pit, and nobody tested gig-goers. But it is the closest evidence we found for the hunch in that forum post.

## What the crowd thinks

Most of the audience, it turns out, agrees with the hunch about other people. In a 2018 survey commissioned by Eventbrite, the ticketing company, and run by ComRes, 1,031 British adults who had been to a live event in the past year were asked about phones.

So seven in ten found filming irritating, and about half did it anyway. Only 13% wanted no-phone zones. Lee Hadlington, a cyberpsychologist at De Montfort University, summed up the double standard: people say a special photo is fine, but "when someone else does it, that's really annoying."

The filming was not only a young person's habit. Among UK gig-goers aged 35 to 44 in 2018, born roughly 1974 to 1983 and mostly Gen X, the share who took photos and videos was as high as among 18 to 24 year olds.

In the US, a 2015 Harris Poll for Ticketfly, another ticketing company, found 31% of smartphone-owning 18 to 34 year olds at live events said they were on their phones for half the show. Just under 15% said they never used their phones at live events.

## The pouch

Some artists have stopped asking. Bob Dylan's 2024 European dates were promoted in partnership with Yondr, the company that locks phones in pouches you carry all night, which would "prohibit the use of cameras and mobile phones during the performances".

In 2026 Phoebe Bridgers made every stop of her Lost Tour phone-free, with Yondr pouches handed out at the door, KQED reported. She said it would feel "dystopian" to be recorded while she cried and sang.

Not every musician agrees. Damon Albarn pushed back against Dylan's ban in July 2024. "If you start banning things where does it end?" he said. "People won't want to be on their phone if you're engaging with them correctly."
