A post in an online Gen X forum shared an article saying we are in worse shape in our fifties than Boomers were at the same age, and the replies split between "obviously" and "says who".

The "who" is mostly Hui Zheng, a professor of sociology at Ohio State University. "The worsening health profiles we found in Gen X and Gen Y is alarming," he said when his study with Paola Echave came out in March 2021. He added a caveat that matters for this piece: "People in Gen X and Gen Y are still relatively young, so we may be underestimating their health problems."

## What the US study actually measured

Zheng and Echave used two big US government surveys: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, with 62,833 people tested between 1988 and 2016, and the National Health Interview Survey, with 625,221 people from 1997 to 2018. They compared generations at the same ages, using blood and urine markers for metabolic syndrome, inflammation and kidney function.

Their finding, published in the American Journal of Epidemiology, was that physical health got steadily worse from the Baby Boomers through Gen X (born 1965–80) and on into Gen Y. Anxiety and depression rose too, generation by generation, among white Americans.

One thing the study did not do is watch Gen X in their fifties. The lab data ran to 2016, when the oldest Gen Xers were about 51 by our arithmetic. "Worse in our fifties" is a reasonable worry drawn from the trend, not a measurement.

## Australia ran the same-age test

In Australia, a research team compared Boomers (born 1946–65) in the 1989–90 National Health Survey with Gen X (born 1966–80) in the 2007–08 survey, both at ages 25 to 44. Rhiannon Pilkington and colleagues published it in PLoS ONE in 2014.

Weight was the clearest gap. Gen X men were almost twice as likely to be obese as Boomer men at the same age. For women the gap was smaller.

The same study asked people to rate their own health. Fewer Gen Xers chose "excellent": 25.1% of Gen X women against 36.3% of Boomer women at the same age.

## The UK: heavier, sooner

Britain has some of the world's longest-running birth cohort studies, following people born in 1946, 1958 and 1970. A 2015 analysis in PLOS Medicine found that the age at which the typical man became overweight fell from 41 for the 1946 cohort to 33 for the 1958 cohort and 30 for those born in 1970. For women it fell from 48 to 44 to 41.

Those born in 1970 are early Gen X. The researchers warned of "severe public health consequences in decades to come".

## What is better: the cigarettes

Here the story turns. In the Australian comparison, Gen X smoked less than Boomers had at the same age: 22.3% of Gen X women against 28.3% of Boomer women. The authors reported no differences in physical activity.

The US picture is murkier. The Ohio State team found the share who had ever smoked rose across generations, yet said smoking could not explain the decline in physical health. Obesity helped explain the rise in metabolic syndrome, but not the rise in inflammation.

Across countries, the cigarette is in retreat. In the UK, 11.9% of adults smoked in 2023, according to the Office for National Statistics, down 8.3 percentage points since 2011. In Singapore, daily smoking fell from 10.6% in 2019 to 8.4% in 2024, Ministry of Health survey figures show.

Weight is moving the other way. The same Singapore survey found obesity among residents aged 18 to 74 rose from 10.5% in 2019–20 to 12.7% in 2023–24. Those figures cover all adults, not one generation, so they show the direction rather than a Gen X verdict.

We could not find a comparable same-age generational study for the Philippines, Canada, Ireland or New Zealand. If you know of one, we would like to read it.

So the honest answer to the forum is: heavier and less likely to call our health excellent than Boomers were at the same age, but less likely to be lighting up. In Australia, Gen X men aged 25 to 44 were obese at nearly double the rate of Boomer men at the same age.
