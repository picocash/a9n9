A couple in an online Gen X forum announced they had both retired, at 58 and 56, with one last alarm clock left to set. In another thread, someone pushed out of work years early admitted, a little sheepishly, to feeling lighter than they had in decades.

Both are retirement. Only one was a choice. Craig Copeland, director of wealth benefits research at the Employee Benefit Research Institute, put the split plainly to USA Today in May 2026: "The higher-income people are more likely to do it because it's their choice." For many others, he said, the timing comes from health or a change at the company. ["It's out of their control."](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/most-us-retire-earlier-planned-090645488.html)

## The plan says 66. The calendar says 61.

Gallup asked Americans about this in April 2026. People still working expect to retire at [66 on average](https://news.gallup.com/poll/709319/nonretirees-worry-remains-high.aspx). That is five years later than the age retirees say they actually stopped. Gallup says it has consistently found a gap of about that size.

The Retirement Confidence Survey, run by EBRI and Greenwald Research and sponsored by financial firms including Fidelity, Prudential and T. Rowe Price, found the same pattern in January 2025: workers' median expected age was 65, retirees' median actual age was [62](https://greenwaldresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2025-rcs-release-report.pdf). Four in ten retirees said they left earlier than planned. Of those, over two in five said they could afford to, but nearly seven in ten named something outside their control.

The Society of Actuaries surveyed 1,005 retirees in 2024. Of those who left early, the top reason was a change in health (31%), then job dissatisfaction (25%), job loss (20%), a change in family situation (19%) and, for 16%, reaching a savings goal sooner than expected. The 58-and-56 couple are in that last group. They are not the biggest one.

## Australia counts it properly

The Australian Bureau of Statistics asks everyone aged 45 and over. In 2024–25, retirees said they had stopped at an average of [57.3](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/labour/employment-and-unemployment/retirement-and-retirement-intentions-australia/latest-release): 60.0 for men, 55.2 for women. That average includes people who retired decades ago. Those who retired in 2024–25 left at 63.8, while people still working intended to go at 65.6.

Why they left matters too. A third (33%) reached retirement age or became eligible for their superannuation. Another 13% stopped because of their own sickness, injury or disability, and 6% were retrenched, dismissed or ran out of work.

## Across the OECD, later than 2000, earlier than the rules

The OECD estimates the average age people actually leave the labour market. In 2024 it was [64.7 for men and 63.6 for women](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_e40274c1-en/full-report/effective-age-of-labour-market-exit_fd1212b7.html). The low point was around 2000, at 62.0 and 59.7. In 1970 it was 66.3 and 64.9.

Set against each country's normal pension age, the picture splits. In 2024 American men left at 67.3, slightly after a normal pension age of 66 and eight months. In the UK, men left at 63.8 against a pension age of 66, and in Australia at 64.9 against 67.

## Singapore just moved the line

Singapore sets the age at which an employer may retire you. From 1 July 2026 the minimum retirement age rose to [64 and the re-employment age to 69](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/publication/articles/32838/retirement-and-re-employment-ages-to-be-raised-to-64-and-69-from-1-july-2026), announced by Minister of State for Manpower Gan Siow Huang in March 2024.

One caveat: the OECD figures are estimates built from labour-force surveys, and the US and Australian numbers are what people say about themselves.

Here is the most striking one. In 2026, nearly half of American retirees, 46%, said they left work earlier than they had planned. Only 6% left later.
