Phyllis Foundis is sandwiched between a 94-year-old mother, who lives independently, and two teenage sons. "I realised that if there were no boundaries then everyone would suffer," she told SBS's *Insight* in May 2025.

Most of us know the set-up. Your mother rings with a view on your son's job, hair or phone. An hour later your son sends a message about his grandmother that you are definitely not forwarding. You are the switchboard.

It is a crowded exchange. In a Pew Research Center survey in September 2025, a quarter (25%) of US adults had a parent aged 65 or older and also a child under 18 or an adult child they had helped financially in the past year. That was true of 54% of Americans in their 40s and 45% of those in their 50s; in Pew's 2021 survey the figure for people in their 50s was 36%.

It isn't all strain. In that 2021 survey, sandwiched adults were a little more likely than others to say they were very satisfied with their family life (48% against 43%).

In the UK, the Office for National Statistics counted about 1.4 million "sandwich carers" aged 16 to 64 in data from January 2021 to May 2023: people with a child at home who also care for a sick, disabled or older relative. Half (51%) were 45 to 64, and 61% were women. Some 31% showed signs of depression or anxiety, against 24% of all adults.

In Singapore, a Singapore Management University panel asked residents aged 48 to 79 about caregiving in November 2024. About one in seven was a caregiver, and 58% of the people they cared for were parents or parents-in-law. Some 45% of the caregivers were also working full-time.
