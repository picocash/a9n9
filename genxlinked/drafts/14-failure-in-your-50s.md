Last month, a woman in her mid-50s wrote a post in an online forum for Gen X women. In 1987 she was 18, told she had her whole life ahead of her, the first in her family expected to make it. She listed what never happened: the family, the partner, the life abroad. She called it coming to terms with failure.

"There's a midlife crisis with a low point around age 50, which was my low point of happiness, too," the economist David Blanchflower told CNBC in 2020. "But there's evidence that, for most people, things do get better from then on."

## The song she can't feel any more

She mentioned a song from the 1980s that annoyed her as a teenager: "Girls Just Want to Have Fun". Now, she wrote, she wishes she could feel it again.

It drew more than 170 comments. That's the part worth reading.

## What the replies said

The first answer, and the most common, was a question back. Plans change; who decided that makes you a failure? Failure, several wrote, lives in the mind of the person doing the judging.

Others separated two things that often get blurred: being alone and being lonely. Several women said the loneliest time of their lives was inside a bad relationship, not living on their own afterwards. Some described a freedom they now value above most things, one most women in history never had.

One described holding on to pain and measuring herself against other people's carefully edited lives. Through therapy, she said, she slowly let go, and began to see the shades of grey between all-or-nothing thinking. And a lot of the replies were short: they had needed to read it that day.

## The curve

Blanchflower, of Dartmouth College, pooled surveys of around half a million people in 132 countries. In his 2020 working paper, well-being bottomed out at about 47.2 in advanced countries and 48.2 in developing ones. "The happiness curve is everywhere," he wrote.

The UK's Office for National Statistics measures it every year. In the year to March 2023, average life satisfaction was lowest among people aged 50 to 54, at 7.25 out of 10. It was 7.71 for people aged 65 to 69.

Not everyone accepts the curve. In a 25-year study that followed one group from age 18 to 43 and another from 23 to 37, Nancy Galambos and colleagues at the University of Alberta in Canada found that happiness generally rose rather than dipped. "I think it's important to question conclusions that have already been drawn about mid-life happiness," she said in 2016. Her study followed the same people as they aged.

That share, 7.9%, was the highest of any age group in the same ONS table. For 65 to 69 year olds it was 5.0%.

## Expectations, and the treadmill

The writer Jonathan Rauch, author of *The Happiness Curve*, argues that the dip is partly about the gap between what we expected and what we got. When we're young, he told *Experience Life* in 2019, we "tend to overestimate how happy we're going to be in midlife as we achieve our goals."

He also described what tends to come after: "Over time, we experience less stress, regret, and emotional volatility."

## When work is the thing that went

For some, the setback is a job. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 3.3 million workers displaced between January 2023 and December 2025 from jobs they had held for at least three years. By January 2026, 72.9% of those aged 25 to 54 were working again, against 57.3% of those aged 55 to 64 and 38.6% of those 65 and over.

In Singapore, about 4,200 professionals, managers, executives and technicians aged 45 to 59 were retrenched in 2025, up from 3,200 in 2024, the Ministry of Manpower told Parliament. Their six-month re-employment rate was 48.5%.

Back to the curve, which is the most hopeful number we found. In the UK, people aged 65 to 69 rated their lives 7.71 out of 10, higher than people in their early 30s, at 7.55.
