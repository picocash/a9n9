A post in an online Gen X forum asked what happened to letting your kids go off to college and become adults.

Today a parent can see where their 19-year-old is at 2am. "Parents who use location tracking think they are keeping their child safe," said Sarah Clark, co-director of the University of Michigan Health C.S. Mott Children's Hospital poll, in June 2026. But, she added, ["our findings suggest that families are split on where that boundary should be."](https://www.powershealth.org/about-us/newsroom/health-library/2026/06/15/half-of-us-parents-track-their-adult-childrens-location)

## Keeping an eye on them

The Mott poll asked 1,542 US parents of 18 to 25 year olds. 52% had tracked their child's location at least occasionally. 96% said the child knew, and 54% said they had given the child the option to opt out.

The parents who don't track had their reasons. 65% said it would feel like an invasion of privacy, and 51% said it might hold back their child's independence. That is the forum post's argument, made by parents.

Pew Research Center's broader survey, fielded in late 2023, looked at parents of 18 to 34 year olds. [A quarter (25%) tracked a young adult child by GPS](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2024/01/ST_2024.01.25_Parents-Young-Adults_Report.pdf), rising to 42% of those answering about an 18 to 24 year old. Daughters were tracked more often than sons, 31% against 21%. 73% texted that child at least a few times a week, and 54% talked on the phone that often. And 71% said their children's successes and failures reflect on the job they've done as parents.

## The kids don't seem to mind

Here's the part that doesn't fit the complaint. In the same Pew survey, 69% of young adults said their parents were about as involved in their day-to-day lives as they'd like.

::: stat
9% | of US young adults said their parents were too involved in their lives | Pew Research Center, 2023
:::

More, 22%, said their parents weren't involved enough.

Money still flows downhill. 59% of parents said they had helped a child aged 18 to 34 financially in the past year, and 44% of young adults said they had received help. Only 45% of young adults called themselves completely financially independent: 16% of 18 to 24s, 44% of 25 to 29s and 67% of 30 to 34s.

## Leaving home: later, but not everywhere

In the US, Pew's Richard Fry found that [18% of 25 to 34 year olds lived in a parent's home in 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/04/17/the-shares-of-young-adults-living-with-parents-vary-widely-across-the-us/), 20% of young men and 15% of young women.

In the UK, [28.7% of 20 to 34 year olds lived with their parents in 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/families/bulletins/familiesandhouseholds/latest), up from 25.4% in 2015, and 34.9% of young men did. In Canada, the 2021 Census found [35% of 20 to 34 year olds living with at least one parent](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/220713/dq220713a-eng.htm), up from 31% in 2001 and unchanged since 2016.

Ireland sits just above the middle. [Eurostat puts the average Irish leaving age at 26.8 in 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/yth_demo_030?format=JSON&lang=en&geo=IE&geo=EU27_2020&geo=FI&geo=HR&geo=IT&geo=DK&geo=SE&geo=ES&time=2024&sex=T&sex=M&sex=F), against [26.2 across the EU](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20250923-1): 27.4 for Irish men and 26.3 for Irish women. In Croatia it is 31.3; in Finland, 21.4. In Ireland, as in the UK and the US, young men stay at home longer than young women.

## Where leaving home has rules

In Singapore, housing policy sets one of the milestones. To buy a Housing & Development Board flat as a single, [an unmarried buyer "must be at least 35 years old"](https://www.mynicehome.gov.sg/get-started/hdb-buying-guide-singles/).

We couldn't find a like-for-like figure for when our own generation left home, so the comparison with "our day" stays anecdotal. The current numbers are clear enough. A quarter of US parents of young adults can see where their child is on a map, and just 9% of those young adults think their parents are too involved.
