A post in an online Gen X forum asked women in their fifties whether they had become invisible, and reply after reply said yes: passed over at the shop counter, talked over in meetings, skipped on dating apps.

None of this is new. In her 2016 book *Everyday Sexism*, Laura Bates wrote that one word kept coming back in the thousands of stories women had sent her project. ["In the case of older women, that word is 'invisible'."](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/187007/1/The_Sexageism_Project_Summary_Report_2022.pdf)

## Someone actually counted the ways

In 2022 Sue Westwood, a researcher at York Law School, ran an online survey of UK women aged 50 and over. After removing spoiled answers, 158 replies were left. Her [paper on the results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37097812/) sorts the invisibility they described into five kinds.

Being missing or misshown in the media. Being treated as sexually undesirable. Being ignored in shops, bars and public spaces. Being "grandmotherised", assumed to be a grandmother whether or not you are one. And being patronised, as if you couldn't possibly know what you're doing.

The examples are small and familiar. A woman of 73 wrote of ["standing waiting to be served in a shop and being completely ignored as if I'm invisible."](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/187007/1/The_Sexageism_Project_Summary_Report_2022.pdf) Another described making a point in a meeting, being ignored, then hearing someone male or younger repeat it and get the credit.

## It isn't only about looks

Westwood calls it "sexageism": ageism and sexism arriving together. The point is that a woman can be noticed for looking older and ignored for everything else at the same time.

The survey wasn't a random sample, so it can't tell us how common this is. It shows what it feels like. For the scale, we need bigger surveys, and those mostly measure ageism without splitting it by sex.

## How big is it?

Globally, very. A [2021 UN report from the World Health Organization and three other UN bodies](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-03-2021-ageism-is-a-global-challenge-un), drawing on a survey of 83,034 people in 57 countries, found that about half of all people hold ageist attitudes.

In the US, the University of Michigan's National Poll on Healthy Aging asked 2,048 adults aged 50 to 80 in December 2019. [More than 80% said they regularly met at least one form of everyday ageism](https://www.futurity.org/?p=2402802), and women were more likely than men to report it.

In Australia, the Human Rights Commission's 2021 report *What's age got to do with it?* surveyed 2,440 people and found ageism looked different at each stage of life. Young adults were talked down to or ignored, people over 60 were "helped" without asking, and [middle-aged Australians were the ones usually turned down for jobs](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/ageism-is-affecting-australian-adults-of-many-ages-new-research-finds/njx59dl8e). "Age is not the problem. Ageism is," said Kay Patterson, the Age Discrimination Commissioner, when the report came out.

## Where the sexes split

The clearest gender figures we found come from Australian Seniors, an insurance brand of Greenstone Financial Services, which surveyed more than 5,000 Australians over 50 in 2024. [Overall, 73% said people become less seen or heard after 50](https://www.seniors.com.au/news-insights/the-australian-seniors-series-gen-seen-report-2024), and 42% felt less visible at work.

Split by sex, the gaps were wide. 74% of women said they had been body-shamed after 50, against 43% of men. 43% of women said a health worker had dismissed their symptoms, against 34% of men.

## Singapore measured it too

The first large-scale ageism study in Singapore, from the National University of Singapore's HELO survey, asked 3,034 people of all adult ages. [Just under half, 49.4%, reported experiences of ageism](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41917534/), with the highest scores among the youngest (21 to 30) and the oldest (71 and over).

Not everyone minds. One of Westwood's respondents, aged 54, wrote that she was relieved sometimes to be invisible, but that it was frustrating at work. Westwood notes that other researchers have found the same mix: relief at escaping the "male gaze", alongside frustration at lost authority.

The UK survey's sharpest line came from a woman of 71, and Westwood used it to title her paper: "it's the not being seen that is most tiresome." A woman of 67 said she now wears more eccentric clothes, because she gets ignored in shops when she wears an ordinary winter coat and hat.
