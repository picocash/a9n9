A post in an online Gen X forum about putting two spaces after a full stop drew thousands of replies, most of them firmly on one side or the other.

Science has looked into it. In 2018, Rebecca Johnson, Becky Bui and Lindsay Schmitt of Skidmore College tracked the eyes of 60 students reading paragraphs with one space or two after each full stop. Their conclusion, in the journal *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*: "we should probably be arguing passionately about things that are more important."

We won't. But we can count.

## Blame the typewriter

Typewriters gave every letter the same width, so typing classes taught a second space to make the end of a sentence easier to see. Plenty of people trained that way kept the habit on computers, which mostly use proportional fonts where the extra space isn't needed.

The style guides can't decide either. The fifth edition of the APA manual asked for one space, the sixth, in 2009, switched to two, and the seventh has gone back to one. "Those who learned to type on an actual typewriter felt vindicated," the Purdue Global Writing Center noted of the 2009 switch.

The Skidmore study found two spaces made the first pass through a text slightly easier for everyone, and comprehension didn't change. Of its 60 readers, 21 were two-spacers. Only they read faster overall with two spaces, and only a little.

::: stat
3% | faster overall reading with two spaces, and only for people who type two | Skidmore College, 2018
:::

In a YouGov poll of 9,138 American adults in April 2020, 56% said they type one space and 34% two. Age makes the difference.

## The full stop that sounds cross

The other half of the thread was about texts. In 2015, Celia Klin's team at Binghamton University showed 126 undergraduates short exchanges ending in a one-word reply such as "Okay" or "Yup". Replies with a full stop were rated less sincere than replies without one, but only as texts. Written as handwritten notes, the full stop made no difference. "Texting is lacking many of the social cues used in actual face-to-face conversations," Klin said. In later work, her team found a reply with an exclamation mark came across as more sincere.

The study tested students, not their parents. Nobody we could find has measured how people over 45 read a text that ends in a full stop.

## Dude

Some threads were about vocabulary. In 2004, University of Pittsburgh linguist Scott Kiesling published a study of "dude" in the journal *American Speech*. He traced its teenage boom to *Fast Times at Ridgemont High*, and said its power was "cool solidarity". His students reported using it least with parents, bosses and professors.

## Thumbs

The Philippines was texting before most of us had a mobile. Filipinos sent an average of 500 million text messages a day in 2006, according to the National Telecommunications Commission, double the 2005 figure.

Emoji have moved on too. Google shared a sample of 665 million emoji typed on its Gboard keyboard with *The New York Times* for a 2026 analysis, with 2025 the latest year. The loudly crying face has overtaken the two laughing faces at the top, largely because young people decided the laughing ones were overused, according to linguists and the paper's analysis of Reddit posts.
