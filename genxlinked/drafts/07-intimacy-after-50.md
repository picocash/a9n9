There is a quiet assumption that intimacy fades out somewhere around the time the reading glasses come in. The research doesn't support that. Several large studies have asked people over 50 about their sex lives, plainly and in confidence, and the answers are more varied, and more hopeful, than the stereotype.

Here is what four of them found. They come from different countries and years and use different age bands, so treat them as snapshots rather than a league table.

## England: most people in their fifties are still sexually active

The English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) is one of the most careful sources on this. In 2012/13, [6,201 people aged 50 and over](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/93296/1/ELSA-SRAQ_Pre-proofs.pdf) filled in a detailed questionnaire about their relationships and sexual health. "Sexual activity" was defined broadly, to include intercourse, masturbation and kissing, petting or fondling.

Among people in their fifties, 94% of men and 76% of women reported some sexual activity in the past year. The figures fell with each decade: in their sixties, 85% of men and 60% of women; in their seventies, 59% and 34%.

The study's own summary puts it simply: activity declines with age, but "a sizable minority of men and women remain sexually active until the eighth and ninth decades of life." Even among the sexually active over-80s, about half the men and 62% of the women reported frequent kissing or petting.

It also found that problems with sexual function were fairly common, but worry about them was much lower. Among sexually active people, the most common concern was level of desire, reported by 15% of men and 11% of women.

## United States: interest outlasts activity

For Americans, the best recent data covers people a little older than Gen X. In a 2018 University of Michigan poll sponsored by AARP, [1,002 people aged 65 to 80](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/05/180503085606.htm) answered questions about sex and relationships.

Forty percent were sexually active. Among those with a partner, it was 54%. Nearly two-thirds said they were interested in sex, more than half said sex was important to their quality of life, and 73% said they were satisfied with their sex life.

There were big differences between men and women. Half of men said they were extremely or very interested in sex, compared with 12% of women. And 84% of men, against 69% of women, said sex was an important part of a romantic relationship.

One more finding stands out: only 17% had talked to a doctor or other health professional about sexual health in the past two years, even though 62% said they would if they had a problem.

## Australia: hope for the future

La Trobe University's *Sex, Age & Me* study surveyed [more than 2,000 Australians aged 60 and over](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2017/06/sex-and-older-australians--it-s-time-we-talked-about-it) and interviewed 53 of them in depth. Seventy-two percent had had sex in the previous year: 76% of men and 61% of women.

More people hoped or planned to have sex in the future than had in the past year: 88% of men and 72% of women.

## Across 29 countries: equality matters

The widest comparison is older but still worth knowing. The Global Study of Sexual Attitudes and Behaviors surveyed about [27,500 people aged 40 to 80 in 29 countries](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/655102), equal numbers of men and women. It was funded by Pfizer and published in 2006.

Satisfaction was highest in Austria, followed by the [United States, Spain and Canada](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/04/060419073758.htm). Japan and Taiwan were lowest. In the Western countries in the study, about two-thirds of men and women said their sexual relationships were satisfying, and 80% were satisfied with their ability to have sex. About half of men and a third of women there said sex was extremely or very important to them.

In the East Asian countries surveyed, about a quarter of men and women reported physical and emotional pleasure from sex. The researchers linked higher satisfaction to countries with more equality between men and women. Across all countries, men reported sexual well-being at least 10 percentage points higher than women did.

The release we read did not give separate figures for the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, the Philippines or Singapore, so we can't place those countries in the ranking.

## What it adds up to

Put the four studies side by side and a few things repeat. Most people in their fifties are still sexually active. Activity tends to fall with each decade, and women report less of it than men at every age; in England, older women were also much less likely than older men to be married or living with a partner. Interest and hope often outlast activity. And in the US poll, far fewer people had talked to a doctor about it than said they would.

None of this is a standard to meet. It is simply a picture of how many people our age are living, which turns out to be a lot more varied than the jokes suggest.

This is not medical advice; talk to your doctor about your own health.
