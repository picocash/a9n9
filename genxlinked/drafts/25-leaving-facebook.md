A post in an online Gen X forum announced a quiet breakup: after almost 20 years, the poster had deleted Facebook. No drama, no farewell post. Just gone.

Plenty of us have thought about it. Fewer have done it. "We might be less engaged, but we haven't walked away because there really isn't an alternative," Carolina Milanesi, an analyst at Creative Strategies, told Agence France-Presse when Facebook turned 20 in February 2024.

It opened on 4 February 2004 as thefacebook.com, for Harvard students only. By 2023 it reported more than 3 billion monthly users.

## Who is still there

In the United States, Facebook's reach has barely moved in a decade. When Pew Research Center first asked, in 2012, 54% of American adults used it. Pew found 68% of American adults used it in 2016 and 70% in its 2026 survey of 5,511 adults, run from January to June. Pew changed how it collects answers in 2023, so the trend is approximate.

By age, the people in their thirties and forties lead. Those aged 50 to 64 are close behind, and well ahead of the over-65s.

The surprise in that table is the youngest row: American adults under 30 use Facebook about as much as people aged 50 to 64. The bigger split in Pew's 2026 figures is between the sexes, with 77% of women using Facebook against 63% of men.

## The long goodbye

Leaving is rarely a clean break. In 2018, Pew asked American Facebook users what they had done in the previous 12 months: 54% had changed their privacy settings, 42% had taken a break of several weeks or more, and 26% had deleted the app from their phone. Almost three in four (74%) had done at least one of the three. The survey ran from 29 May to 11 June 2018, so this is the mood of eight years ago.

The app-deleters skewed young. Some 44% of users aged 18 to 29 had removed it from their phones, compared with 12% of those 65 and over. Pew didn't publish the figure for the ages in between.

## Quieter, not gone

In Britain, the change looks more like going quiet than going away. Ofcom's survey of 7,533 adults, run from September to November 2025, found that 49% now post, share or comment on social media, down from 61% a year earlier. The rest mostly like posts or read without joining in. Nine in ten internet users still use at least one platform. Only 59% thought the benefits of being online outweighed the risks, down from 72% the year before.

That fits Milanesi's point about being less engaged.

## Where Facebook is still growing

Then there is the Philippines. Meta's advertising tools could reach 95.8 million people there in October 2025, according to DataReportal's Digital 2026 report. That is 81.9% of the whole population, and 8.95 million more than a year earlier, a rise of 10.3%.

Those are advertising numbers, not a count of users, and DataReportal warns they can run high: the figure for adults alone works out at 122.1% of the adult population, which can't literally be true. Even so, the direction is clear.

Singapore is cooler. Facebook ads could reach 3.80 million people there in late 2025, or 64.6% of the population and 75.5% of adults, up 5.6% in a year.

So the reader who left after almost 20 years is in good company, but not a crowd. In the Philippines, meanwhile, Facebook's reach grew by almost nine million people in a single year.
