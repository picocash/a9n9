The e-reader was supposed to finish off the paperback. It didn't, but it did move in.

"In 2025 we were more likely to listen to a book, or to read content digitally, than ever before," said Dan Conway, chief executive of the UK's Publishers Association, when it published its 2025 figures in June 2026. The same figures showed print still brought in £2.1 billion of the £2.6 billion UK consumer publishers earned. Both things are true at once: the shelf is crowded, and the phone is full.

## The paperback is still the default

In Canada, BookNet Canada, the book industry's non-profit data agency, surveyed 1,979 English-speaking adults in July and December 2025. Print made up 76% of the books they bought. E-books were 14% and audiobooks 7%.

Paperbacks alone were nearly half of everything.

## Midlife readers, by format

The Pew Research Center asked 8,046 US adults in October 2025 what they had read in the past year. Among 50 to 64 year olds, 61% had read a print book, 26% an e-book and 23% had listened to an audiobook.

The surprise is at the young end: 18 to 29 year olds were the most likely to have read a print book (66%), and also the most likely to have read an e-book (41%). It's hard to be smug about that.

## Singapore: print slipping, e-books holding

Singapore's National Library Board runs a National Reading Habits Study every three years. The 2024 edition surveyed 3,725 adults aged 20 and over: 75% read physical books, 55% e-books and 14% listened to audiobooks.

Physical reading has fallen. Singapore's Ministry of Digital Development and Information told Parliament in September 2025 that it "dropped to 75% in 2024, compared to 89% in 2018," while e-book reading held steady. Among readers aged over 60, 35% now use e-books.

## Down under, the ears have it

Creative Australia's National Arts Participation Survey of 9,065 people, fielded from June to September 2025, found 66% of Australians read physical books, the same as in 2022. E-books slipped from 41% to 36%. Audiobooks rose from 34% to 37%, so they now edge out e-books.

New Zealand shows the same tilt. Read NZ's 2025 National Reading Survey found 87% of adults had read a book in the past year. The biggest jump was audiobooks: 43%, up from 31% in 2021.

"It counteracts many of the international narratives we hear about reading for pleasure in decline," said Juliet Blyth, Read NZ's chief executive.

The surveys ask different questions of different age groups, so treat this as a set of snapshots, not a league table. Wherever we found format figures, print is still ahead. And in Canada, nearly half of all the books people bought in 2025 were plain paperbacks.
