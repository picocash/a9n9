You buy an air fryer. It makes chips. Then it asks for your location and permission to use your phone's microphone.

That's what the UK consumer group Which? found in November 2024, when it tested three app-connected air fryers: all three wanted to know customers' precise location and asked for permission "to record audio on the user's phone, for no specified reason". One wanted your gender and date of birth to set up an account. Harry Rose, editor of *Which?* magazine, said manufacturers were able to collect data "seemingly with reckless abandon, and this is often done with little or no transparency."

## The word for it

In November 2024, Australia's Macquarie Dictionary made "enshittification" its Word of the Year, and the public vote agreed with the committee. Its definition: "the gradual deterioration of a service or product brought about by a reduction in the quality of service provided", especially of an online platform, as a consequence of profit-seeking.

The committee said the word "captures what many of us feel is happening to the world." Many of us feel it most in the kitchen.

## We are not the refuseniks you'd expect

Here's the awkward bit for the stereotype. In a Pew Research Center survey of US adults in February 2026, 36% of 50 to 64 year olds said they had a smart speaker, slightly more than adults under 30 (33%). People in their 30s and 40s were the keenest (41%); those 65 and over the least (24%).

Smartwatches are another matter: 35% of Americans aged 50 to 64 had one, against 44% of those under 50.

## Ireland: the "no need" answer

Ireland's Central Statistics Office asked internet users in 2024 whether they used any internet-connected home devices: energy management, security systems, smart appliances, voice assistants. Among 45 to 59 year olds, 37% used none.

The CSO then asked the non-users why. Most of them said they had no need, and that held across ages: 66% of 45 to 59 year olds, and 78% of 16 to 29 year olds. Privacy concerns were named by 24% of the 45 to 59s.

So a lot of young people don't want a smart kettle either. That's not a generation gap. It's a kettle.

## Switching it off at the wall

In the UK, a survey for the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, run by London Economics and YouGov in August and September 2023 with 3,352 adults, counted what people actually own. Smart TVs were in 64% of hands, smart speakers 38%. Smart washing machines: 8%. Smart fridges: 3%.

It also asked how often people turn off a device's internet connection. Of the over-65s, 13% said always, compared with 3% of 18 to 24 year olds. In the same research's app survey, 70% of 55 to 64 year olds worried about their data being sold to third parties, against 52% of 18 to 24s.

## "Watching and capturing my every action"

In Singapore, researchers from Nanyang Technological University and A*STAR gave smart home sensors to 42 people aged 55 and over between 2018 and 2019. Most (31) kept them. Of the 11 who didn't, nine said they saw no benefit.

A few raised privacy. "It is watching and capturing my every action," one woman, 67, told the researchers. Another participant, 70, thought her neighbours had it wrong: "They thought it was an infringement of their privacy, but actually it is not."

We looked for someone who counts smart toothbrushes by age. Nobody does, as far as we could find, which may be the best news in this piece.

What's left is the air fryer. Asked about the microphone permission, Xiaomi told Which? that it "is not applicable to Xiaomi Smart Air Fryer". The app asked anyway.
