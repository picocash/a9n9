The story goes like this. We knocked on doors. We rode bikes until the streetlights came on. We called a friend's house on the landline and talked to their mum first. So our friendships must have been deeper than anything built through a screen.

Part of that story is true. The bigger claim, that it gave us better friendships for life, is much harder to prove. Here is what the evidence actually says.

## The freedom part is real

Children who grew up in the 1970s and 1980s really did have more freedom to roam, at least in England, where it was measured. A long-running study by the Policy Studies Institute and the University of Westminster asked parents whether their primary school children were allowed to travel home from school alone. In 1971, [86% of parents said yes](https://road.cc/content/news/73654-childrens-independence-outside-home-has-dramatically-dropped-over-last-40-years). By 1990 it was 35%. By 2010 it was 25%.

That covers the childhoods of Gen X fairly well: the oldest were starting school in 1971, the youngest were children around 1990. It is the strongest piece of evidence that our childhoods were more independent than those that followed.

What it doesn't show is whether that freedom produced better friendships.

## Friendship has shrunk, for everyone

In the United States, there is a striking comparison. In 1990, a Gallup survey found that 3% of Americans had no close friends and 33% had ten or more. In 2021, the Survey Center on American Life asked a similar question and found [12% with no close friends and 13% with ten or more](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/).

That looks like a collapse. But the researchers flag a catch: the 1990 survey was done by phone and the 2021 survey online, and that difference may affect the answers. It also describes all adults, not one generation. Gen X were among those adults in 2021 too.

The same 2021 survey found that young adults were in some ways more socially active than seniors. 56% of 18 to 29 year olds had made a new friend in the past year, compared with 41% of seniors. And 61% of young adults had texted a friend in the past day.

## Are young people today lonelier than we were?

A little, according to the best long-term evidence. Researchers at Ruhr University Bochum pooled 345 studies covering 124,855 young adults, all measured on the same loneliness scale, between 1976 and 2019. They found that [loneliness among young adults increased slightly](https://news.rub.de/english/press-releases/2021-12-17-psychology-loneliness-among-emerging-adults-increased-slightly) over those 43 years.

The key word is slightly. The authors called the popular term "loneliness epidemic" overdramatising, because the effect size was relatively small. They also said young adults should not be overlooked when designing ways to tackle loneliness.

So the nostalgic claim gets a small amount of support here: young adults in the 1970s and 1980s, which includes older Gen X, were on average a bit less lonely than young adults in 2019. A bit, not dramatically.

## How Gen X is doing now

This is where the story gets less comfortable. If Gen X had built unusually strong friendships, you might expect us to be among the least lonely adults today. In the US, the opposite shows up.

AARP research reported in December 2025 found that [40% of Americans aged 45 and older were lonely](https://www.inquirer.com/health/loneliest-americans-survey-loneliness-increasing-20251207.html), up from 35% in both 2010 and 2018. The loneliest group was people aged 45 to 49, at 49%. That is younger Gen X.

In the UK, the age pattern runs the same way. Analysis of ONS survey data by the Campaign to End Loneliness found that between November 2022 and February 2023, [9.7% of 16 to 29 year olds felt lonely often or always](https://www.campaigntoendloneliness.org/press-release/younger-brits-report-higher-levels-of-loneliness/), along with 8.2% of 30 to 49 year olds, compared with 3.7% of people over 70.

Globally, a Meta-Gallup survey of 142 countries and areas found that [27% of people aged 19 to 29 felt very or fairly lonely](https://news.gallup.com/opinion/gallup/512618/almost-quarter-world-feels-lonely.aspx), compared with 17% of those aged 65 and over. Most people aged 45 and older said they did not feel lonely at all.

Pew Research Center found a similar age gradient for close friends in the US. Among those aged 65 and over, [49% had five or more close friends](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/10/12/what-does-friendship-look-like-in-america/). For those aged 50 to 64 it was 40%, for 30 to 49 it was 34%, and for under-30s 32%.

## So, did we have better friendships?

The honest answer is that the evidence doesn't show it. Our childhoods were freer, at least in England. Young adult loneliness has crept up a little since the 1970s. But across countries, the pattern that stands out most is about age, not birth year: people tend to feel less lonely and report more close friends as they get older. Gen X, in the middle, sits in the middle.

What does hold up is how much friendship matters. In Pew's 2023 survey, 61% of Americans said having close friends is extremely or very important for a fulfilling life. That was far more than said the same about being married (23%) or having children (26%).

The street corner may be gone, but the reason we went there hasn't changed.
