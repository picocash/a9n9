A post in an online Gen X forum recalled the one piece of advice the older women in the family all gave: whatever happens, keep a bank account in your own name. The replies filled up with mothers, grandmothers and aunts who had said the same.

They had their reasons. In the early 1970s, Emily Card applied for a credit card in her own name. "I was turned down," she told NPR in 2024. She was told that "if I wished for my husband to apply, he could."

## What the law used to allow

Card later worked in Washington for a senator who attached a short provision to an unrelated bill. It became the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, passed in 1974: "just a couple of sentences about not discriminating against women on account of sex or marital status," as she put it. Before it, said Elizabeth Babcock of the Smithsonian American Women's History Museum, "You actually had to have another male relative, usually, cosign for any kind of access to credit or a credit card." The law has since been widened to cover age, race and other grounds.

Britain followed a year later. Section 29 of the Sex Discrimination Act 1975 made it unlawful to refuse a woman goods, facilities or services on the grounds of her sex, and its examples include "facilities by way of banking or insurance or for grants, loans, credit or finance". Before then, the NatWest archive notes, "Many banks required married women to submit a form of authorisation signed by their husband".

In Ireland the turning point was work rather than banking law. Until the marriage bar was lifted in 1973, women in the civil service and other jobs had to leave when they married, and husbands typically held the only account, *The Irish Times* noted in 2013. Australia's federal Sex Discrimination Act came in 1984, and its definition of services includes "banking, insurance and the provision of grants, loans, credit or finance".

So the women giving the advice had, in many cases, lived through a time when the account could only be his.

## How couples do it now

In the US, most couples keep at least some money apart. Bankrate, a personal-finance site, commissioned YouGov to survey 2,564 American adults in December 2025, 1,208 of them married or living with a partner. Of the couples, 38% combined everything, 36% had a mix of joint and separate accounts, and 26% kept everything separate.

The generations split sharply. Among Gen X (aged 46 to 61 in the survey), 40% fully combined their money, 37% had a mix and 23% kept it all separate. That makes 60% of Gen X couples with at least some money of their own. Among Gen Z, more than half kept their finances completely separate.

## Where it shows up later: pensions

Money of your own is one thing. Money in retirement is another, and the gap there is wide. The OECD compares the average retirement income of women and men aged 65 and over who receive a pension. Across 34 countries, women received 23.7% less than men on average. In Japan the gap was 47.3%. The UK's was 37.9%, the US 31.3% and Ireland's 30.4%. Canada's was 18% and Australia's 13.1%. The OECD had no figure for New Zealand.

Singapore shows the same pattern in its Central Provident Fund. In 2020, among Singaporeans aged 55 to 70 who had not yet reached the basic retirement sum and were still contributing, women had a median S$90,000 in their accounts and men S$133,000, the Ministry of Manpower told Parliament.

The Philippines runs the other way on one measure. In the central bank's 2025 Consumer Finance and Inclusion Survey, women continued to outpace men in account ownership, the *Daily Tribune* reported, even as the share of adults with an account fell to 50% from 56% in 2021.

The advice in the family was about the bank. The gap that lasts is in retirement: across 34 OECD countries, women aged 65 and over with a pension receive close to a quarter less than men, and in Japan close to half.
