A post in an online Gen X forum asked a simple question, separate bedrooms or not, and hundreds of couples answered: the snorers, the night-sweaters, the early risers, the ones who read until two, and the ones who swore they'd never.

The people who study sleep tend to be relaxed about it. "Although the term 'sleep divorce' seems harsh, it really just means that people are prioritizing sleep," Dr Seema Khosla, a North Dakota pulmonologist and spokesperson for the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, said when the academy published [its survey in July 2023](https://www.upi.com/Health_News/2023/07/13/couples-sleep/4691689253251/).

## How many of us do it

The academy asked 2,005 American adults in March 2023. One in five (20%) said they occasionally slept in another room to accommodate a bed partner, and 15% said they did so consistently. Men were far more likely to be the ones who moved: about 45% of men did so at least occasionally, against about 25% of women.

The generations differed too. Gen X sat in the middle, at about a third.

In Britain, YouGov's 2022 sleep study found that 79% of people in a relationship share a bed with their partner and [18% don't](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/42963-yougov-sleep-study-part-two-sleep-habits). Among those aged 60 and over, the share not sharing rose to more than one in four.

## Around the world

ResMed, which sells sleep-apnoea machines, surveyed 30,026 people in 13 markets in December 2024, including the US, UK, Australia, New Zealand and Singapore. It found that [18% of couples](https://d1io3yog0oux5.cloudfront.net/_65fda8accb243564886fedc734854f95/resmed/news/2025-02-26_ResMed_s_Fifth_Annual_Global_Sleep_Survey_Reveals__395.pdf) had permanently opted to sleep apart, citing snoring and restlessness. Among those who slept separately, 31% said their relationship had improved and 30% said it had got worse.

Singapore stands out. In the same survey, as reported by theAsianparent, 49% of Singaporean couples had [tried sleeping separately](https://sg.theasianparent.com/resmeds-2025-global-sleep-survey-singapore), and 64% of them reported better sleep. That counts anyone who has tried it, not only couples who stuck with it, so it isn't comparable with the global figure.

## Does it last?

Fewer stick with it than the headlines suggest. Sleep Doctor, the company that owns SleepFoundation.org, surveyed 1,250 Americans who regularly shared a bed in January 2023. Just [1.4% had started sleeping apart and kept it up](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-news/sleep-divorce-versus-sharing-a-bed) for the past year. Of those who had tried it, 25.7% ended up back in the same bed. The most common reason: they missed each other (34.9%).

## What the sleep lab found

Sharing a bed is not only a cost. In a small German study published in Frontiers in Psychiatry in 2020, 12 young couples spent four nights in a sleep lab, some together and some apart. Together, their REM sleep [increased and was less disrupted](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/735652), even though their bodies were more restless. "One could say that while your body is a bit unrulier when sleeping with somebody, your brain is not," said Dr Henning Johannes Drews of the Center for Integrative Psychiatry in Germany, one of the researchers.

The caveat is the size: 12 couples, all young, and none of them in their fifties.

So the surveys and the lab point in slightly different directions. Bodies are more restless together, brains may be calmer, and of the Americans who tried a separate room in 2023, a quarter came back because they missed each other.
